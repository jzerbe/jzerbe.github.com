__Result:__ You can protect your home, but only with lead time.
Either buy long-term care insurance, or get your assets out of your name at least 5 years before applying for Medicaid long-term care.
Exceptions exist if you have a surviving spouse, a child under 21, or a child who is permanently disabled or blind.

## The Problem
This is a uniquely American problem.
It would be hilarious if it were not so effective at enforcing the US caste system.

The [Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993](https://oig.hhs.gov/oei/reports/oei-07-92-00880.pdf)
requires state governments to recover long-term care benefits paid by Medicaid after the recipient dies.
Yes, the state can take your home by filing a lien against it
[if you are unable to return home](https://www.senioranswers.org/insurance/medicaid/estate-recovery/).
It can also go after other property titled in your name when you die: your (probate) estate.

### Exceptions
A state's Medicaid program will not come after your estate if you have:

- A surviving spouse
- A child under age 21
- A child who is permanently disabled or blind

See the [Colorado](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/colorado/10-CCR-SS-2505-10-8-063) and
[Florida](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0409/Sections/0409.9101.html)
laws for reference. If one of those applies, stop reading. You're good to go.

## The Fix

### Option 1: Avoid Medicaid long-term care
If you can, buy a
[long-term care insurance plan](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/insurance/long-term-care-insurance)
in your mid 50s or 60s so you never end up in Medicaid estate recovery.
A [premium of a few thousand dollars a month](https://smartasset.com/insurance/how-much-does-long-term-care-insurance-cost)
may be out of reach for some.

### Option 2: Move the assets out of your name
Do this at least [5 years before applying for Medicaid long-term care benefits](https://www.medicaidplanningassistance.org/medicaid-look-back-period/).
Retitle your assets into a business entity or a trust.
Any [gifts or asset transfers](https://coloradoestateplan.com/why-asking-how-do-i-avoid-the-5-year-medicaid-lookback-period-leaves-you-at-risk/)
inside that 5-year window count as a penalty against your benefit.

__If you use a trust:__ You need a trustee, who will take a management fee.
The most efficient route is to [find a lawyer](https://www.licensedlawyer.org/)
who will both draft the trust documents and act as trustee.
The main choice is [revocable vs. irrevocable](https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/071615/what-difference-between-revocable-trust-and-living-trust.asp).
A revocable trust can be changed later. An irrevocable trust cannot, and it comes with some estate tax benefits.

__If you use an LLC:__ This works if you already trust a family member or close friend.
Create an [LLC yourself](/2020/08/colorado-llc/) or have a
[registered agent company do it for you](https://www.coloradoregisteredagent.com/colorado-registered-agent-services/).
Have them set up a manager-managed LLC, get a tax ID, and file your annual report.
The single member and initial manager is your friend or family member.
They report the LLC's expenses and revenue on their taxes, and done right, they can take hefty deductions.

Then:

1. Sell the assets to the business or trust below market value ($1?) and close with a
[warranty deed](https://www.deedclaim.com/warranty-deed/). This lets the new owner use a
[stepped-up tax basis](https://www.homelight.com/blog/can-i-sell-my-house-to-a-family-member/).
2. Get a real estate insurance policy for the business or trust through [National Real Estate Insurance Group](https://nreig.com/).
3. If you plan to keep living in the home, have the business contract a [local property manager](https://www.deerwoods.com/lakewood-property-management).
That gives you room to set up a zero-dollar lease.
If not, use a full-service company like [Nomad Lease](https://nomadlease.com/products) to manage everything.

### What if I have a mortgage?
Most mortgages have an "acceleration" or "due on sale" clause.

> This means that when you transfer a mortgaged property out of your name, the entire mortgage balance is due.
> 
> ...
> 
> However, this is not usually a problem when we are not selling your house but, rather, transferring it for estate planning purposes.
> In fact, federal law specifically prohibit a mortgage holder from exercising a "due on sale" clause on
> residential real property containing less than five dwelling units or on a cooperative apartment unit when the property is transferred to a spouse
> or children of the owner, or a lifetime revocable or irrevocable trust where the borrower retains the right to occupancy.
<https://davidowlaw.com/listen-lawrence-answer-irrevocable-trust-mortgage-question/>

This rule came from banks not wanting borrowers to keep a lower interest rate through a sale.

- Transfer or sell your home to your children while keeping your right to live there,
and the lender cannot enforce the "due on sale" clause: <https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1701j-3>
- In Colorado, your interest rate cannot go up by more than 1% for a sale: <https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/2021/title-38/article-30/section-38-30-165/>
